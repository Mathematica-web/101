🏗️ Conditions for a Parallelogram

The four specific rules that force a generic quadrilateral to lock into a parallelogram!

Proving the Shape

You don't need to measure every single line and angle to prove a shape is a parallelogram. Mathematicians have proven that if a quadrilateral satisfies just one of the following four conditions, it automatically guarantees all the others.

Theorem The Condition
Theorem 8.3 If each pair of opposite sides is equal, then it is a parallelogram.
Theorem 8.4 If each pair of opposite angles is equal, then it is a parallelogram.
Theorem 8.7 If the diagonals bisect each other (cut exactly in half), then it is a parallelogram.
Theorem 8.8 If exactly one pair of opposite sides is BOTH equal and parallel, it is a parallelogram.

📏 1. Opposite Sides Equal (Thm 8.3)

Adjust the lengths of the top and right sides. Watch how forcing Top = Bottom (250) and Right = Left (150) snaps Vertex C into the exact position required to form a perfect parallelogram!

Adjust lengths to match opposite sides.

💡 Why does this work? (The Proof)

Imagine drawing a diagonal line from A to C. This splits the quadrilateral into two triangles: ΔABC and ΔCDA. Since opposite sides are equal (AB=CD, BC=DA) and they share the diagonal (AC=CA), the triangles are congruent by the SSS (Side-Side-Side) rule. Because the triangles are perfectly identical, their alternate interior angles match, which mathematically proves that the opposite sides must be parallel!

📐 2. Opposite Angles Equal (Thm 8.4)

In this shape, the top and bottom lines are kept perfectly horizontal. Adjust the top-left and top-right vertices. When you successfully make Angle A = Angle C, notice how Angle B instantly matches Angle D to form a Parallelogram!

Angles are unmatched.

💡 Why does this work? (The Proof)

The sum of all angles in a quadrilateral is 360°. If we force opposite angles to be equal (Let A=C and B=D), then A + B + A + B = 360°. This simplifies to 2(A + B) = 360°, which means A + B = 180°. When consecutive interior angles add up to exactly 180°, transversal geometry rules (Euclid's 5th Postulate) dictate that the lines forming those angles must be perfectly parallel!

✖️ 3. Diagonals Bisect (Thm 8.7)

Here, the intersection point (M) is fixed. Adjust the sliders to center M precisely in the middle of both diagonals (50%). When both diagonals are bisected, the outer boundary forms a perfect Parallelogram!

Align both to 50% to bisect.

💡 Why does this work? (The Proof)

Look at the crossing diagonals at point M. They create two triangles on opposite sides (e.g., ΔAMB and ΔCMD). Vertically opposite angles at M are equal. If the diagonals bisect each other, the side segments are equal (AM=CM and BM=DM). By the SAS (Side-Angle-Side) rule, the opposing triangles are perfectly identical. Since they are identical, their alternate interior angles match, proving the outer lines are parallel!

📏 4. One Pair Equal & Parallel (Thm 8.8)

The final shortcut! If you know that the top and bottom sides are perfectly parallel (Tilt = 0°) AND perfectly equal in length (250 units), the side walls are mathematically forced to close into a Parallelogram!

Adjust length to 250 and tilt to 0°.

💡 Why does this work? (The Proof)

Imagine a diagonal connecting Vertex A to C. Because the top and bottom lines are parallel, the alternate interior angles they form with the diagonal are identical. Now we have two triangles sharing a diagonal side, an equal interior angle, and we *forced* the top and bottom sides to be equal. By the SAS rule, the two triangles are congruent. Therefore, the remaining pair of sides (AD and BC) are forced to be equal and parallel too!

🧠 Test Your Knowledge

Q1: If a quadrilateral has opposite sides measuring 10 cm and 12 cm, can it be a parallelogram?
No!

According to Theorem 8.3, for a quadrilateral to be a parallelogram, each pair of opposite sides must be completely equal in length. Because 10 ≠ 12, it fails the condition and the shape will warp into a generic quadrilateral or trapezium.
Q2: If the diagonals of a shape measure 14 cm and 20 cm, and they cut each other at 7 cm and 10 cm respectively, is it a parallelogram?
Yes!

This satisfies Theorem 8.7. Since 7 is exactly half of 14, and 10 is exactly half of 20, both diagonals perfectly bisect each other. This condition guarantees the shape is a parallelogram.
