📏 Criteria for Congruence of Triangles

You don't need to check all 6 parts to prove triangles are identical!

The Minimum Requirements

A triangle has 6 main parts (3 sides and 3 angles). To prove two triangles are perfectly congruent, mathematicians discovered you only need to match 3 specific parts in the correct order. If those 3 parts match, the remaining 3 parts are magically forced to match as well!

Congruence Rule What it Means
SAS (Side-Angle-Side) Two triangles are congruent if two sides and the included angle of one triangle are equal to two sides and the included angle of the other.
ASA (Angle-Side-Angle) Two triangles are congruent if two angles and the included side of one triangle are equal to two angles and the included side of the other.
AAS (Angle-Angle-Side) A variation of ASA: If any two angles and a non-included side match, the triangles are congruent (because the third angle must also match).

🔒 1. The SAS Locker

In SAS, the angle must be "included" (sandwiched directly between the two known sides). Notice how fixing Side c, Side b, and Angle A leaves only one possible line to form the third side. The triangle is completely locked!

Required 3rd Side Length Forced length = 218px Because two sides and the included angle are fixed, it is mathematically impossible to draw Side a any other way!

📐 2. The ASA Intersection

In ASA, the side is "included" (sandwiched between two known angles). Watch how setting the base Side c and fixing the trajectory of Angle A and Angle B forces their rays to intersect at exactly one point (Vertex C).

Rays intersect perfectly to form a unique triangle.

⚠️ 3. The SSA Ambiguity Trap

Why isn't Side-Side-Angle (SSA) a valid rule? Because if the angle is not included between the two sides, the third side can snap into two different places. Toggle between the two triangles to see the ambiguity!

Triangle 1 (Obtuse Base) Both triangles have the exact same Angle A, Side c, and Side a. Yet they are completely different shapes! This is why SSA is invalid.

🧠 Test Your Knowledge

Q1: Can AAA (Angle-Angle-Angle) be used to prove two triangles are congruent?
No!

AAA proves that triangles are similar (having the same shape), but they could be completely different sizes! Think of an equilateral triangle—it always has 60° angles, but it could be the size of a coin or the size of a pyramid. You need at least one Side (S) to lock in the scale.
Q2: Why is AAS a valid rule, but not SSA?
AAS is just ASA in disguise. Because the angles of a triangle always sum to 180°, if you know two angles, you automatically know the third! So AAS instantly turns into ASA, which we know works perfectly to lock a triangle. SSA, however, creates an ambiguous swinging side that can make two completely different shapes.
